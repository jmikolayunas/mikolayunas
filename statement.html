Artist Statement

My work begins in systems. Given any object, place, or process, my attention goes first to the forces at work inside it — what is moving, what is resisting, what is giving way, and on what timescale. Water finding its course, land rising and wearing away, a body moving through terrain: these share a single logic of pressure, resistance, and release.

I know that logic through my body before I know it through analysis. On a mountain bike, nearly all of my attention lives in the rear wheel — its traction, its trajectory, my weight feeding it exactly the grip it needs — while the line I choose follows the path of least resistance through the terrain. In those moments I am not observing the physics; I am inside them. The studio works the same way. A resin pour is felt through viscosity, surface tension, and timing rather than calculated. Decisions arrive as sensations before they arrive as reasons.

I work forward from fascination rather than from a thesis. An image forms, and I go to whatever lengths are required to build it — researching the systems, materials, and procedures the piece demands, absorbed in that problem-solving completely. Meaning accrues through the making and settles afterward; the finished object is the record of that sustained attention. The waterfalls I built in school came from this same instinct: a way to sit with moving water long enough to understand it, and to hold what I saw.

The current work translates elevation and bathymetric data into relief sculpture in hardwood and resin. The resin's translucency is structural to the idea, exposing the seafloor — its densities, its tidal pressure — so each piece becomes a record of forces acting across timescales we seldom consider: how a place was formed, what is reshaping it now, and where it is headed. The forms follow nature's geometry, eased edges and continuous transitions; nature produces no straight lines and no right angles, and the work honors that.

A second thread runs through the work: our attachment to place. A landscape becomes part of how we understand ourselves, even as we occupy it for a moment while it continues on a timescale indifferent to our presence. We dwell in places until they dwell in us, and still they continue as if we were never there. When I look at the finished pieces I see the forces that shaped the place. Collectors often see a place they love. Both readings are true, and the work is built to hold both.

Wood, resin, and topography are the current vocabulary for this inquiry, not its conclusion. If the study of motion, force, and timescale leads to different materials or subjects, I intend to follow it as a continuation of the same practice.

Building this work is the most direct way I have of standing inside these questions rather than only thinking about them. That is less a choice than a condition of how I work.
