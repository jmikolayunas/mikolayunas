## 1. The Private Artist Statement
*(For your own use — process notes, grant/gallery applications, press kit. Fuller and more exploratory than the site needs. Trim as you see fit.)*

I default to thinking in long timescales. Given any object or place, I find myself asking what forces made it, and where those forces are still taking it — not where it *is*, but where it's *headed*. That habit of mind is the actual origin of the work, more than any particular material or subject.

I'm drawn to physics — fluid dynamics especially, and anything kinematic. Not the mathematics of it, but the visible logic: water finding the path of least resistance, motion resolving toward smoothness and efficiency. Mountain biking is where I feel this most directly. Threading a line through terrain, at speed, is an exercise in becoming water — reading the ground the way a current reads a riverbed, and finding the smoothest way through it. I'm not thinking about the trail. I *am* the path of least resistance, briefly.

Geology is the same principle on a scale I'll never live long enough to see move. A cliff face, a moraine, a river delta — these read as static, but they're not finished. They're mid-motion, operating on a clock too slow for a human nervous system to register directly. What looks frozen is actually still traveling — no beginning, no end, just a very long middle. I've spent most of my working life trying to catch that middle and hold it still long enough to look at.

That's the paradox at the center of everything I make: capturing motion by freezing it. It's a contradiction, and I don't think it resolves — I think the work *is* the attempt, over and over, from a different angle each time. The freestanding waterfalls I built in school were a direct version of this: studies of water's motion, arrested. Carcinogenesis extended the idea forward — I built in the certainty that the piece would eventually decay past the point of functioning, so the motion didn't stop at the studio door. The piece kept going after I was done with it.

The ocean and coastline work — the topobathy pieces in wood and resin — grew out of the same drive, for a few reasons I can name clearly:

1. Researching a place's geologic history is a way of understanding a system in motion — how it was built, and where it's going.
2. The finished piece lets me expose that motion at once: evidence of the past forming, the present relationship with the sea that's actively reshaping it, and an implied direction for what comes next. Past, pressure, and trajectory, visible in a single object.
3. Composition — knowing intuitively what to include, crop, and emphasize — is something I'm genuinely good at without having to think hard about it, and I don't want to lose sight of that as a real skill, separate from the subject matter.
4. The resin pour itself is physical, hands-on fluid dynamics. Viscosity, surface tension, cure time — I have to understand and manipulate the actual physics of a fluid to get the piece right. The material process mirrors the subject matter.

Wood, resin, and topography are not where this stops. They're the material and subject I've been using to build the skillset and vocabulary I need — a stop along the way, not the destination. If the drive to study motion and long-timescale systems leads somewhere else — a different material, a different subject entirely — I want to be able to follow it without treating that as abandoning who I am as an artist. The engine is the interest in time and motion. The topographic landscape work is one expression of it, not its definition.

There's a second, related thread I keep circling: our relationship to place, and how much of our sense of self we quietly borrow from it. We tend to feel possessive of the places that shape us, without registering that we're only ever borrowing them for a while — the place will outlast us by orders of magnitude. I notice that the instinct to attach a personal story to a landscape is partly a genuine question I'm still working through, and partly a reflex that serves the commission model — a client connects to a piece through their story, and that's a real and valid thing for the work to do. But I don't think that story is the reason I make the work. It's the reason someone else *wants* it. Those are two different truths, and I don't need to force them into one.

I don't have a tidy single sentence that unifies all of this yet, and I'm not sure I need one. What I know is that the work is an ongoing study of motion made visible across a timescale otherwise imperceptible — using whatever material and subject currently gives me the clearest way to study it.

## 2. About Page Copy (Public-Facing, Luxury-Positioned)

*Written to gallery-wall restraint: declarative, no outcome claims, no "handcrafted," no question marks. Designed to slot into the existing `about.html` structure — this can replace or sit alongside the current "Founder Story" copy block.*

**Suggested placement: opens the Founder Story section, ahead of or replacing the current first two paragraphs.**

> The work begins with a question of motion. A coastline, a mountain range, a stretch of seafloor — each one reads as fixed, but none of it is finished. Every landform is mid-process: shaped by forces that are still active, moving toward a future state on a timescale too slow to observe directly.
>
> The practice is an attempt to make that motion visible. Elevation and depth data reconstruct a place's geologic history with precision. Wood, resin, and hand-finishing hold it still, briefly, in a single object — evidence of where a place has been, and where it is going.
>
> Each commission begins with a place that matters to someone. The research into that place's formation, its present relationship with the sea or the land around it, and its likely future is what gives the work its structure. The story arrives afterward, carried by the collector.

*(This keeps the "place and memory" narrative — which is doing real work for the commission model — but grounds it in the more precise, less generic idea of geologic motion rather than the current "engineering meets memory" framing, which reads a little more like every other landscape-art site.)*

**Suggested new philosophy-grid entry**, alongside the existing four ("Place as Identity," "One-of-One," "Data Meets Artistry," "Built to Last") — replacing or supplementing "Data Meets Artistry":

> **In Motion, Not At Rest**
> A finished landform is not a finished process. Each piece is built from the same logic that shapes the land itself: water and geology moving toward the path of least resistance, over a timescale rendered visible for the first time.

## 3. Where This Thread Can Surface Elsewhere (Without Over-Explaining)

Fine art doesn't narrate itself — the philosophy grid and About page can carry the *idea* of motion-across-time; everywhere else should just let it be felt.

- **Piece captions**: no change needed to your existing fragment-caption system ("Seafloor detail," "Bathymetric detail" already work), but if you ever add a longer per-piece note, "Still forming" or "Mid-formation" type language fits your existing luxury tone better than anything explaining the process.
- **Process page**: this is the one place on the site where the *how* is allowed to show. A single line connecting the CNC/data precision to "the same forces that shaped the place" would tie the process narrative back to the artist statement without needing the full philosophical framing.
- **Artist Statement as a standalone**: worth keeping the fuller private version (Section 1) out of the public site entirely, or offering only a trimmed version if a gallery or press outlet specifically asks for an artist statement document — collectors buy the object and the shorter story; press and galleries sometimes want the deeper one.
- **Future material pivot**: if you ever move off wood/resin/topography, this framing (motion across imperceptible timescales, currently expressed through geologic form) is portable. It doesn't box you into landscape work the way "sculptural relief artist working in topography" currently does as a self-description.

If that split doesn't feel right — if the place/memory/borrowing theme actually feels just as central to you, not just client-facing — it's worth saying so, because the About page framing above leans on that split being real.
