## 1. The Artist Statement (Institutional)
*(For submission and review — grant panels, gallery packets, press. This is the version that lives at statement.html.)*

My work begins in systems. Given any object, place, or process, my attention goes first to the forces at work inside it — what is moving, what is resisting, what is giving way, and on what timescale. The physics of motion is the layer of the world that reads most clearly to me, and the one I trust most: water finding its course, land rising and wearing away, a body moving through terrain. These share a single logic of pressure, resistance, and release.

I know that logic through my body before I know it through analysis. On a mountain bike, nearly all of my attention lives in the rear wheel — its traction, its trajectory, my weight feeding it exactly the grip it needs — while the line I choose follows the path of least resistance through the terrain. In those moments I am not observing the physics; I am inside them, briefly indistinguishable from matter moving through the world according to its laws. The same embodied attention governs the studio. A resin pour is felt through viscosity, surface tension, and timing rather than calculated. Decisions arrive as sensations before they arrive as reasons.

I work forward from fascination rather than from a thesis. An image forms, and I go to whatever lengths are required to build it — researching the systems, materials, and procedures the piece demands, absorbed in that problem-solving completely. Meaning accrues through the making and settles afterward; the finished object is the record of that sustained attention, and once complete, it belongs to its own continuation. The waterfalls I built in school came from this same instinct: a way to sit with moving water long enough to understand it, and to hold what I saw.

The current work translates elevation and bathymetric data into relief sculpture in hardwood and resin. The resin's translucency is structural to the idea, exposing the seafloor — the varying densities of material, the interaction with tidal force — so that each piece becomes a snapshot of an ecosystem of forces acting across timescales we seldom consider. Researching a place's geologic history means entering a system in motion: how it was formed, what pressures are reshaping it now, and where it is headed. The finished piece holds all three at once. The forms themselves follow nature's geometry — eased edges, continuous transitions. Nature produces no straight lines and no right angles; the work honors that.

A second thread runs through the work: our attachment to place. A landscape becomes part of how we understand ourselves — it roots us, steadies us, becomes something we would defend as if it were our own body. Yet we occupy it for a moment, while the place travels on beneath our feet, in motion too slow to perceive and indifferent to our brief presence. We dwell in places until they dwell in us, and still they continue as if we were never there. That overlap of timescales — deep attachment measured in decades, set against ground measured in epochs — is where the work's human meaning lives. When I look at the finished pieces I see the forces that shaped the place. Collectors often see a place they love. Both readings are true, and the work is built to hold both.

Wood, resin, and topography are the current vocabulary for this inquiry, not its conclusion. If the study of motion, force, and timescale leads to different materials or subjects, I intend to follow it as a continuation of the same practice.

Building this work is the most direct way I have of standing inside these questions rather than only thinking about them. That is less a choice than a condition of how I work.

## 2. About Page Copy (Public-Facing, Luxury-Positioned)

*Written to gallery-wall restraint: declarative, no outcome claims, no "handcrafted," no question marks. Designed to slot into the existing `about.html` structure — this can replace or sit alongside the current "Founder Story" copy block.*

**Suggested placement: opens the Founder Story section, ahead of or replacing the current first two paragraphs.**

> The work begins with a question of motion. A coastline, a mountain range, a stretch of seafloor — each one reads as fixed, but none of it is finished. Every landform is mid-process: shaped by forces that are still active, moving toward a future state on a timescale too slow to observe directly.
>
> The practice is an attempt to make that motion visible. Elevation and depth data reconstruct a place's geologic history with precision. Wood, resin, and hand-finishing hold it still, briefly, in a single object — evidence of where a place has been, and where it is going.
>
> Each commission begins with a place that matters to someone. The research into that place's formation, its present relationship with the sea or the land around it, and its likely future is what gives the work its structure. The story arrives afterward, carried by the collector.

*(This keeps the "place and memory" narrative — which is doing real work for the commission model — but grounds it in the more precise, less generic idea of geologic motion rather than the current "engineering meets memory" framing, which reads a little more like every other landscape-art site.)*

**Suggested new philosophy-grid entry**, alongside the existing four ("Place as Identity," "One-of-One," "Data Meets Artistry," "Built to Last") — replacing or supplementing "Data Meets Artistry":

> **In Motion, Not At Rest**
> A finished landform is not a finished process. Each piece is built from the same logic that shapes the land itself: water and geology moving toward the path of least resistance, over a timescale rendered visible for the first time.

## 3. Where This Thread Can Surface Elsewhere (Without Over-Explaining)

Fine art doesn't narrate itself — the philosophy grid and About page can carry the *idea* of motion-across-time; everywhere else should just let it be felt.

- **Piece captions**: no change needed to your existing fragment-caption system ("Seafloor detail," "Bathymetric detail" already work), but if you ever add a longer per-piece note, "Still forming" or "Mid-formation" type language fits your existing luxury tone better than anything explaining the process.
- **Process page**: this is the one place on the site where the *how* is allowed to show. A single line connecting the CNC/data precision to "the same forces that shaped the place" would tie the process narrative back to the artist statement without needing the full philosophical framing.
- **Artist Statement as a standalone**: worth keeping the fuller private version (Section 1) out of the public site entirely, or offering only a trimmed version if a gallery or press outlet specifically asks for an artist statement document — collectors buy the object and the shorter story; press and galleries sometimes want the deeper one.
- **Future material pivot**: if you ever move off wood/resin/topography, this framing (motion across imperceptible timescales, currently expressed through geologic form) is portable. It doesn't box you into landscape work the way "sculptural relief artist working in topography" currently does as a self-description.

## 4. Confirmed Hierarchy

After several rounds, this is the settled structure:

- **Artist engine:** motion, time, geologic force, flow.
- **Collector bridge:** place, memory, return.
- **Material chapter:** wood, resin, topography, as the current vocabulary.

This is now reflected in both the statement above and the live `about.html` Founder Story copy.

**Left on the table, not resolved:** whether to ever explicitly situate the work in dialogue with other artists working on time, ecology, or impermanence (e.g. for a grant narrative or panel bio) — that's a different, more deliberate move than anything folded into the statement itself, and worth doing on purpose if the occasion calls for it rather than reflexively.
