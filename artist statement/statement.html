Artist Statement

My work begins with an attention to motion that exceeds ordinary perception. Water finding its course, land rising and wearing away, coastlines shifting, a body moving through terrain — these forces share a single logic of pressure, resistance, and release. I recognize that logic most directly on a mountain bike, threading a line through terrain at speed: reading the ground the way a current reads a riverbed, finding the smoothest way through without stopping to think about it. For a few seconds, I am the path of least resistance.

Geology runs on the same logic, at a scale no single life is long enough to watch move. A cliff face, a moraine, a river delta, a stretch of seafloor may read as fixed, yet each is still becoming — held, for now, inside a much longer passage of change. My own process holds that same duality in miniature: the pour, the carving, the hours of hand-finishing are immediate and active, while the finished object sits still, apparently at rest, carrying that activity inside it as evidence.

The waterfalls I built in school were an early version of this instinct — direct studies of water's motion, arrested. The ocean and coastline work grew from the same drive. Researching a place's geologic history means understanding a system in motion: how it was built, and where it's headed. The finished piece exposes that motion at once — evidence of the past forming, the present pressure reshaping it, and an implied future, held in a single object. Composition, deciding what to include, crop, and emphasize, is a skill I bring to this independent of subject matter, and one I intend to keep even if the subject changes.

A second thread runs underneath all of this: how little a human life weighs against the timescale of a place, and how deeply we attach to it anyway. In the brief span we occupy a landscape, it becomes part of how we understand ourselves — an anchor that feels permanent from the inside, though the place was never really ours to keep. The instinct to attach a personal story to a landscape is part genuine question, part reflex that serves the commission model. The client's story isn't why I make the work. It's why someone wants it. Both are true, and they don't need to collapse into one.

Wood, resin, and topography are the current vocabulary for this inquiry, not its conclusion. If the study of motion and timescale leads to a different material or subject, I intend to follow it as a continuation of who I am as an artist.

Building this work is the most direct way I have of standing inside that question rather than only thinking about it. That isn't a choice I make each morning. It's closer to a condition of how I work.
